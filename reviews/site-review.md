# Independent rendered site review

**Verdict: fix the exhibit’s direction before shipping.** The desktop and phone captures otherwise support the reading-first approach. This review covered six opening, chronology and expanded-detail screenshots, the generated public copy, the four exhibit records and their rendering logic, and all 46 final event titles and summaries. The reported 320px text enlargement and Safari keyboard issues are separate checks for the release owner.

- The exhibit stacks its input above its destination on desktop and phones, but the arrow points right, toward neither box. In the blocked state it also resumes after the barrier with an arrowhead, which implies that something still reaches the destination. These are contradictory cues in the one diagram that teaches visitors how the exhibit works. Point the route toward the next box, and end the blocked route at the barrier. Keep a distinct continuation cue for the HTTP permission case because a person can choose to proceed.
- In that HTTP case, “Approved sites are remembered, and local addresses are excluded” reads more broadly than the entry. The entry explains that approvals expire after two weeks without a visit and that local addresses and intranets are generally exempt. Use “Chrome remembers recent approvals and generally skips the warning for local addresses and intranets.” For the relay case, “Unauthorised relay refused” risks suggesting the relay itself was refused. “Relaying refused by default” describes the message path and matches the adjacent Sendmail copy.

The reading layout works at both captured widths. The date rail sits apart from the text on desktop and comes before the title on the phone. Headings, sources and disclosure stay in the same order. The paper and ink treatment gives the dark exhibit a clear place without asking people to interact before they can read. The mobile opening shows the case controls near the bottom of the first screen, with the history jump above them. The wide date rail does expose awkward date labels in the first few entries. “1986–1992” is easier to scan than the first entry’s three-line label about Brain, prototypes and the retail market. Its summary and milestone list already explain those dates. Era placement follows the chosen milestone, so the 2017–2021 Flash entry in the 2020s is defensible. The existing chronology note explains varying adoption but could also say what determines the grouping.

The opening earns “We used to trust that” with a floppy disk and the “Enable Content” button. The summaries generally hold that voice where the evidence allows it, particularly the poisoned DNS answer that leaves the expected address in the browser, and the app that asks again when it needs your location. A few visible labels have more polish than meaning. “The network reached every desk” overstates the history, and “Fewer decisions left to a warning” is harder to decode than the paragraph below it. “Windows gets new defaults” and “Changes you didn’t have to turn on” are more concrete alternatives if the era titles are revised. The Flash detail block also shifts into instructions (“Install a plug-in”; “Remove browser support”) even though it records what happened. Make these past-tense statements when revising the exhibit’s source entry. These are small editorial fixes. No broad rewrite is needed.
