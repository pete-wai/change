# Luna omission research: 1980-2005

Research cutoff: 2026-11-06. This pass looks for changes in ordinary security practice, not a chronology of attacks. Dates below distinguish an observed threat, a recommendation or standard, a product rollout, and the evidence date. The parent lead researcher is covering open SMTP relays, so that case is recorded as an overlap and not proposed as another public entry.

## Strong candidates and decisions

| Brain and boot-sector viruses; scanning and removable-media handling | Recommend a bounded public entry on scanner availability and practice, not universal adoption. | The IBM-PC boot-sector virus Brain appeared in 1986. ESET's retrospective says its founders wrote a DOS detection program in 1987 and began retail sales in Austria in 1990; these are one vendor's product-origin and market-entry dates. NIST's 1989 guide recommends screening new software with antivirus tools and controlling disks. Its December 1992 selection guide says a variety of tools were then available and compares their functionality, practicality, and convenience. Together the documents show scanner availability and formalized selection practice by 1992, not the first antivirus product or an adoption date. | The case gives genuine 1980s origins and a bounded shift from local scanner development to documented product availability and federal selection guidance. NIST recommended the controls; its guidance is not evidence that most organizations or home users followed them. ESET is a single vendor and its retrospective cannot establish market-wide adoption. Avoid claims that antivirus became ubiquitous in the 1980s or that one product was first. A later 1996 IBM research article says its neural-net recognition system shipped to millions through IBM AntiVirus, but that establishes distribution of one product feature, not early market share. | NIST, *Computer Viruses and Related Threats: A Management Guide* (1989), full 52-page document: https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.500-166 (virus mechanics and practice recommendations, especially ch. 5); NIST, *A Guide to the Selection of Anti-Virus Tools and Techniques* (December 1992), full document: https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-5.pdf (states a variety of tools were available and discusses scanner selection); ESET, “Celebrating 30 Years of Continuous Progress and Innovation”: https://www.eset.com/sg/about/newsroom/press-releases1/awards/celebrating-30-years-of-continuous-progress-and-innovation/ (vendor retrospective on 1987 detection program and 1990 Austrian sale); NIST, *History of Viruses*: https://csrc.nist.rip/publications/nistir/threats/subsubsection3_3_1_1.html (Brain, disk exchange, DOS virus evolution). |
| Morris worm and coordinated incident response | Recommend public entry. | On 1988-11-02, the Morris worm overloaded connected systems and disrupted communications. DARPA's post-incident review led it to ask Carnegie Mellon University's Software Engineering Institute to establish a central coordination point; the CERT Coordination Center was established in 1988. The sources differ on the month, so this record uses the year only. The worm's date is the incident, while CERT/CC's establishment is the institutional response, not a date when a later global CSIRT network was already in place. | Initially a research and government Internet response, later a model and source of expertise for incident-response teams. Counts vary by denominator and estimate: contemporary/retrospective sources report about 4,000 infected systems or about 5-10% of roughly 60,000-80,000 hosts. Avoid presenting a single rate as settled. The strongest causal evidence is CMU/SEI's institutional history and an SEI report. | CMU/SEI, “Fostering Growth in Professional Cyber Incident Management”: https://www.sei.cmu.edu/history-of-innovation/fostering-growth-in-professional-cyber-incident-management/ (directly states DARPA asked SEI to establish CERT/CC after the worm); CMU/SEI, *Tracking and Tracing* (2002), full report: https://resources.sei.cmu.edu/asset_files/SpecialReport/2002_003_001_13928.pdf (dates CERT's creation to December 1988 and reports incident counts); Carnegie Mellon University, “U.S. Department of Homeland Security Announces Partnership with Carnegie Mellon's CERT Coordination Center” (2003): https://www.cmu.edu/cmnews/extra/030915_cyberpartner.html (calls the center formed in November 1988); CISA, *The President's Commission on Critical Infrastructure Protection Internet Report* (1999): https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/publications/Network%20Group%20Internet%20Report_1999.pdf (reports approximately 4,000 computers/about 5%; cites its own sources). |
| Password sniffing and SSH | Recommend public entry. | Before SSH, remote terminal and file-copy protocols such as Telnet and rlogin sent credentials over unencrypted network paths. Tatu Ylönen developed SSH in 1995 after a password-sniffing incident at his university; the initial implementation was distributed in 1995. The IETF published SSH as a standards-track protocol in January 2006. Those are separate origin, distribution, and standardization milestones. | SSH became a widely used secure remote-login protocol across Unix-like systems and later other platforms, but RFC publication alone does not quantify uptake or mean that insecure protocols vanished. The origin story is Ylönen's own retrospective; avoid calling SSH the first encrypted login protocol. | SSH Communications Security, “History of SSH”: https://www.ssh.com/academy/ssh (creator's retrospective on the 1995 sniffing incident, development, and distribution); IETF RFC 4251 (Jan. 2006): https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4251.html (standards-track architecture, security properties, and host-key limitations); OpenSSH project history: https://www.openssh.org/history.html (OpenBSD integration and public-release history, beginning 1999). |
| Perimeter firewalls as a network boundary | Conditional candidate; core practice is established, but avoid a simple one-incident origin story. | Organizations used packet filters and gateway firewalls to separate networks with different trust levels by the 1990s. NIST's January 2002 firewall guide updates a 1980s-era SP 10 introduction and discusses deployment, selection, policy, and management. A 2009 NIST retrospective says early firewalls were installed at network perimeters, then describes adding internal placement as attacks between internal hosts bypassed the perimeter. | Organizational practice, with no single adoption date or universal prevalence measure. NIST documentation confirms that it was an established field; it does not show when a majority of organizations installed one. The causal story about the perimeter's limits is a later design evolution, not proof that a specific attack caused it. | NIST SP 800-41 (Jan. 2002): https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/41/final (field guide, successor to SP 10); NIST ITL Bulletin (Oct. 2009): https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/itlb/2009/10/protecting-information-systems-with-firewalls-revi/final (explicit historical account of perimeter placement and internal blind spots); IETF RFC 2979 (Oct. 2000): https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2979.html (documents that firewall behavior had become common enough to require interoperability guidance; informational, not prevalence data). |
| Intrusion detection systems | Recommend, with narrower claims. | Perimeter filters decide what traffic to allow; they do not by themselves identify every attack that passes or originates inside. NIST's 1999 guidance on acquiring and deploying IDS and its November 2001 SP 800-31 describe systems that monitor host or network events for signs of security problems. The 2001 date is federal guidance, not an invention or universal adoption date. | Best framed as organizational monitoring becoming a recognized layer alongside access control. NIST's 2001 guide calls IDS a necessary addition to most organizations' security infrastructure, which is an institutional assessment, not an independent deployment census. The technology's research origins predate 1990. | NIST SP 800-31 (Nov. 2001): https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/31/final (full guidance and stated organizational role); NIST, “Acquiring and Deploying Intrusion Detection Systems” (Nov. 1999): listed in NIST's archived 2004 authentication-guideline bibliography, https://csrc.nist.gov/csrc/media/projects/800-63-4/documents/sp800-63-1.pdf (contemporaneous federal guidance existed by 1999); NIST SP 800-94 (2007): https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/94/final (later descriptions of network-, host-, and wireless-based systems; useful for scope, outside this entry's milestone date). |
| Melissa and executable attachment security | Recommend public entry, dated to response rollout. | Melissa appeared on 1999-03-26 as a Word macro document that used Outlook to send itself to address-book contacts. After Melissa and the ILOVEYOU worm of May 2000, Microsoft announced its Outlook Email Security Update on 2000-05-15. The update warned about and restricted access to potentially unsafe attachments; a later Outlook release preserved controls but allowed configuration. Incident date and announcement date are distinct from broad deployment. | This changed behavior in Microsoft Outlook installations where the security update was installed, not in every email client or mail gateway. The existence of later configurable settings and continuing attachment-based malware shows limits. Microsoft's response announcement explicitly names Melissa and ILOVEYOU; do not claim either event alone caused every attachment policy. | Microsoft, “Alert on Macro Virus Issues” (1999-03-30): https://news.microsoft.com/source/1999/03/30/alert-on-macro-virus-issues/ (contemporaneous threat mechanics); FBI, “Melissa Virus”: https://www.fbi.gov/history/cases-and-criminals/melissa-virus (recipient-facing spread and investigation); Microsoft, Outlook security update announcement (2000-05-15): https://news.microsoft.com/source/2000/05/15/microsoft-to-deliver-major-outlook-security-solution-to-help-protect-customers-against-computer-viruses/ (explicitly names Melissa and ILOVEYOU and describes attachment warning/restriction). |
| DES replaced by AES for federal cryptographic protection | Recommend public entry. | EFF's DES Cracker recovered a DES key in less than three days in July 1998; EFF and Distributed.net then won the DES Challenge III in 22 hours 15 minutes in January 1999. These demonstrations showed practical exhaustive key search. NIST published AES as FIPS 197 on 2001-11-26; the standard took effect on 2002-05-26. On 2005-05-19, NIST withdrew DES FIPS standards because DES no longer provided the needed protection and encouraged federal organizations to use AES. | Strong evidence for federal standards and phaseout. The EFF demonstration preceded AES selection and is relevant pressure, but it did not alone cause AES development or adoption; NIST began its AES competition in 1997. Distinguish federal approval from universal replacement; triple DES remained approved for some applications after DES withdrawal. | EFF, original DES Cracker record: https://w2.eff.org/Privacy/Crypto/Crypto_misc/DESCracker/ (July 1998 under-three-day result and Jan. 1999 22-hour-15-minute distributed result); NIST FIPS 197, AES (Nov. 26, 2001; effective May 26, 2002): https://csrc.nist.gov/files/pubs/fips/197/final/docs/fips-197.pdf (full standard); NIST, DES FIPS withdrawal announcement (May 19, 2005): https://csrc.nist.gov/News/2005/Withdrawal-of-FIPS-46-3-FIPS-74-and-FIPS-81 (scope and explicit replacement recommendation). |
| Monthly security bulletin cadence (“Patch Tuesday”) | Recommend as a Microsoft-specific operational change. | In 2001 Code Red and Nimda exploited flaws for which patches had already been available, exposing the gap between patch availability and installation. Microsoft announced on 2003-10-01 that it would move to a monthly security bulletin schedule, after administrators asked for predictable timing. The date marks a vendor's process change; it is not a universal industry-wide patching standard. | Strong evidence that one major software vendor changed release cadence and that customers received a predictable planning rhythm. It does not establish faster installation, higher patch compliance, or that worms alone prompted the change. Keep this distinct from Windows Automatic Updates, which were separately rolled out. | NIST SP 800-40 (Sept. 2002): https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-40.pdf (Code Red/Nimda and delayed patching); Microsoft Security Response Center, “10 years of Update Tuesdays” (Oct. 14, 2013): https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/msrc/blog/2013/10/10-years-of-update-tuesdays/ (Oct. 1, 2003 announcement and customer request for predictability); Microsoft, “Outlines New Initiatives in Ongoing Security Efforts” (Oct. 9, 2003): https://news.microsoft.com/source/2003/10/09/microsoft-outlines-new-initiatives-in-ongoing-security-efforts-to-help-customers/ (contemporaneous rationale and tools). |
| Windows XP SP2 built-in firewall default | Recommend public entry. | Before XP SP2, Windows XP's Internet Connection Firewall existed but was disabled by default. Windows XP SP2 changed the built-in firewall to enable by default when no third-party firewall was detected. Microsoft publicly described it as protection against Blaster and Sasser, which had exploited exposed network services. Microsoft released SP2 to manufacturing on 2004-08-06 and to consumers later that month; do not conflate those dates. | Large Windows XP user base gives this a plausible broad consumer effect, but enterprise policy, existing third-party firewalls, disabled services, exceptions, and operating-system versions varied. Microsoft's statement supports its design rationale, not a measured reduction in infection rates. | Microsoft, Will Poole speech at Worldwide Partner Conference (July 2004): https://news.microsoft.com/speeches/will-poole-microsoft-worldwide-partner-conference-2004/ (explicit threat-to-default rationale); NIST SP 800-69 (2005), full Windows XP Home security checklist: https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-69.pdf (documents pre-SP2 off default and SP2 on default, with a third-party-firewall exception); Microsoft SP2 release-to-manufacturing announcement (Aug. 6, 2004): https://news.microsoft.com/source/2004/08/06/microsoft-releases-windows-xp-service-pack-2-with-advanced-security-technologies-to-computer-manufacturers/ (release milestone). |
| Data Execution Prevention / NX hardware support | Recommend public entry, tightly scoped. | Stack and heap overflows often tried to execute injected bytes from writable memory. AMD's NX and Intel's XD processor features allowed hardware to mark memory pages non-executable; Windows XP SP2 added hardware-enforced DEP support in 2004. Microsoft describes required processor, BIOS, and OS support, so this was not a software-only universal switch. | A defense-in-depth change on compatible processors and supported Windows configurations. On 32-bit XP, DEP policy commonly protected Windows components by default and users could opt other programs in; 64-bit native programs had broader default protection. DEP raises the cost of some exploits but does not eliminate memory-corruption attacks. | Microsoft, “Determine if hardware DEP is available” (support requirements): https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/troubleshoot/windows-client/performance/determine-hardware-dep-available (processor, BIOS, and XP SP2 requirements); Microsoft MSDN Magazine, “Security Tips: Minimizing the Code You Expose to Untrusted Users” (Nov. 2004): https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/msdn-magazine/2004/november/security-tips-minimizing-the-code-you-expose-to-untrusted-users (SP2 DEP, buffer-overflow rationale, companion /GS mitigations); AMD, “Enhanced Virus Protection” (2004): https://www.amd.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2004-2-16-amd-introduces-enhanced-virus-pro.html (processor feature rollout; vendor explanation). |
| WEP to WPA/WPA2 | Recommend public entry as a standards and product transition; do not imply completed replacement in 2004. | WEP's weaknesses were demonstrated in published cryptanalysis in 2001. The Wi-Fi Alliance introduced WPA as an interim package in October 2002 while IEEE completed 802.11i. IEEE approved 802.11i on 2004-06-24 and published it on 2004-07-24; WPA2 was the Alliance's certification label for the full security amendment. | Stronger standards and interoperable certification made better wireless security available, but did not force every access point or user to upgrade. WEP remained in old devices and networks for years. The NIST publication describing WPA2 is later than the standard, so the 2004 dates come from IEEE and the Alliance's own chronology. | IEEE, 802.11i-2004 record: https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/802.11i/3127/ (approval and publication dates; security mechanisms); NIST, *Guide to Intrusion Detection and Prevention Systems*, draft rev. 1 (2012), full PDF: https://csrc.nist.gov/files/pubs/sp/800/94/r1/ipd/docs/draft_sp800-94-rev1.pdf (summarizes WPA's Oct. 2002 introduction and WPA2 label after ratification); Fluhrer, Mantin & Shamir, “Weaknesses in the Key Scheduling Algorithm of RC4” (2001): https://www.cs.cornell.edu/people/egs/615/RC4.pdf (cryptanalytic pressure on WEP's use of RC4). |

## Recommended public entries

1. Morris worm to CERT/CC (incident response coordination, 1988).
2. SSH for remote login (1995 origin; 1999 OpenBSD integration; 2006 standardization).
3. Perimeter firewall and the later recognition of its blind spots (1990s-2002 guidance), if the UI can hold an ongoing practice rather than a crisp incident date.
4. IDS as a recognized organizational monitoring layer (1999-2001 guidance), with prevalence qualification.
5. DES retirement and AES as federal standard (1999 demonstration; 2001 adoption/2002 effective date; 2005 withdrawal).
6. Melissa/ILOVEYOU and Outlook attachment restrictions (1999 threat; 2000 update announcement and rollout).
7. Microsoft monthly security bulletin cadence (announced 2003-10-01; Microsoft-scoped).
8. XP SP2 built-in firewall default (manufacturing Aug. 6, consumer release later Aug. 2004; qualify exceptions).
9. Hardware DEP/NX in Windows XP SP2 (2004; hardware and policy limits).
10. WEP to WPA and WPA2 (2001 attack; 2002 interim product program; IEEE publication July 2004; transition was gradual).

Antivirus/floppy history is suitable for a bounded entry: Brain's 1986 origin, one scanner program by 1987 and retail sale by 1990, 1989 NIST guidance, and the 1992 NIST guide documenting a variety of available products. The available evidence does not establish broad adoption. Keep those origin, product availability, recommendation, and prevalence claims separate.
